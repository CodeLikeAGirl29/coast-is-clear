The Emerald Coast is one of the strongest short-term rental markets in the country — strong seasonal demand, healthy average daily rates, and a steady pipeline of investors who want a piece of it. It's also one of the more regulatory-layered markets to get right, because the rules genuinely change depending on which side of an invisible line your specific address sits on. A condo in Destin, a house on Okaloosa Island a mile away, and a property in what everyone casually calls "Destin" but is actually Miramar Beach can each answer to a different set of taxes, licenses, and restrictions. Get the jurisdiction wrong before you buy, and the investment math you ran doesn't hold.

Here's the layered system, accurately, as it stands right now.

---

### The State Framework: Preemption, and the Bill That Almost Changed Everything

Florida's foundational short-term rental law is **F.S. § 509.032(7)(b)**. It prevents cities and counties from banning vacation rentals outright or regulating how long or how often a unit can be rented — with one major exception: local ordinances that were already restricting rentals **before June 1, 2011** are grandfathered in and remain fully enforceable. This creates real variation between municipalities that had rules on the books early and those that didn't.

In 2024, a bill (CS/SB 280) attempted to go much further — centralizing vacation rental regulation almost entirely under the state DBPR and significantly curbing local control. It passed both chambers of the legislature and was then **vetoed by Governor DeSantis on June 27, 2024**. No comparable bill passed in the 2025 or 2026 legislative sessions, so the current framework — state preemption plus a local patchwork, largely unchanged since 2014 — is what actually governs your purchase today. Legislative attempts to change this keep surfacing, so this is worth a quick re-check before any STR purchase closes, not something to assume is permanently settled.

Separately from local rules, every whole-unit short-term rental in Florida needs a **state vacation rental license from the DBPR** (Fla. Stat. §§ 509.241–509.242) if it's rented for stays under 30 days more than three times a year, or advertised as regularly available for that. This applies regardless of which platform you use, or whether you use one at all. Apply through myfloridalicense.com.

### Jurisdiction Is Everything

This is the part that catches investors who are shopping by "area" rather than by parcel.

**City of Destin:** Short-term rentals aren't allowed everywhere inside city limits — Destin restricts them to a defined set of zoning districts, not the whole map. Every STR needs a City of Destin Business Tax Receipt, and single-family homes and townhomes additionally need a specific STR registration (condos and apartments are exempt from that particular registration step, but still need the Business Tax Receipt). The city also requires a designated "Responsible Party" reachable for emergencies quickly, enforces occupancy limits, and sets quiet hours overnight. Registering late carries escalating penalty fees, so this isn't a step to put off after closing.

**Okaloosa Island:** This stretch sits in **unincorporated Okaloosa County**, not the City of Destin and not Fort Walton Beach — meaning it follows county rules, not either city's ordinance. Okaloosa Island also has its own recorded **Zone B-1 protective covenants**, a separate layer of restriction specific to that area, on top of whatever general county STR rules apply. Don't assume Destin's rulebook travels across the bridge.

**"Destin" that's actually Miramar Beach:** This is the one that trips up more buyers than any other single fact in this article. A lot of property marketed and colloquially referred to as being "in Destin" — particularly along the eastern stretch — is actually in **Walton County**, not Okaloosa County. Walton County requires its own separate Vacation Rental Certificate and runs its own tourist tax structure entirely apart from Okaloosa County's. Before writing an offer on anything near that boundary, confirm the county the parcel actually sits in — don't rely on the neighborhood's common name.

### The Tax Stack

Short-term rental income on the Emerald Coast is taxed in layers, and missing one doesn't make it go away — it just means you find out later, with penalties attached.

1. **Florida state sales tax** — 6% on transient rental income (F.S. § 212.03), plus a small county discretionary surtax, remitted to the Florida Department of Revenue.
2. **Okaloosa County Tourist Development Tax** — the county's ordinance sets this at 6% within the original taxing subdistrict and 5% within the county's "expansion area," so the exact rate depends on precisely where the property sits. Verify the applicable rate and district against the county's current map rather than assuming a flat number.
3. **Registration matters, and don't assume the platform has it covered.** Okaloosa County has stated directly that it does not have a blanket agreement with Airbnb, Vrbo, or similar platforms to collect and remit Tourist Development Tax on an owner's behalf — unlike some other Florida counties. Register and confirm your own remittance obligation at okaloosatouristtax.munirevs.com rather than assuming your booking platform is handling it.

All in, a reasonable planning estimate for the combined tax load on gross short-term rental revenue in this area lands somewhere around 12–13% — verify current rates before finalizing any pro forma, since both the state surtax and the county's district-based rate are the kind of thing that can shift.

### HOA and Condo Rules Live Completely Outside State Law

This is the single most misunderstood layer, and it matters enormously for Destin's condo-heavy STR market specifically: **state preemption does not touch HOA or condominium association governing documents.** If a condo declaration prohibits short-term rentals or sets a minimum lease term, that restriction is fully enforceable regardless of what the city or county otherwise allows.

One nuance worth knowing as a buyer: under Florida condominium law, a declaration amendment that newly restricts or bans rentals generally only binds owners who consented to it or who purchased **after** the amendment's effective date. That cuts both ways — it means you inherit whatever the *current, recorded* declaration says at the moment you close, not an older, friendlier version an agent might describe from memory. Read the actual, current declaration before making an offer on any condo you intend to run as an STR, not a summary of it.

### The Homestead Trap

If you're converting a property you've been living in and homesteading into a short-term rental, don't skip a step: Okaloosa County explicitly flags that a homesteaded property that's no longer your permanent residence — including one converted to a rental — can trigger a homestead exemption violation, potentially clawing back **multiple years** of the tax savings the exemption provided. If you're repositioning a former primary residence into an STR, formally remove the homestead exemption first, rather than letting the county catch it later.

### Running the Real Numbers

Before you fall in love with a projected gross rental number, run it through the full stack: the ~12–13% combined tax load above, property management fees (commonly 20–30% of gross for full-service management on this coast), STR-specific or commercial insurance (a standard homeowner or condo policy typically excludes business/rental use entirely — this is a separate policy conversation, not a rider), HOA dues, and realistic off-season vacancy. A gross rental estimate that looked compelling on a listing sheet can look considerably different once every layer above has taken its cut.

---

### The Bottom Line

None of this makes Destin or Okaloosa Island a bad short-term rental market — it remains one of the stronger ones in the state. It does mean the regulatory homework has to happen at the parcel level, not the neighborhood level: confirm the county, confirm the zoning district, read the actual current HOA declaration, and verify your tax registration obligations directly rather than assuming a platform or a previous owner already handled it.
