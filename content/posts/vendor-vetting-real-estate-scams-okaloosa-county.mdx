A real estate transaction isn't just you and a house. Between contract and closing, you'll hand off money, personal information, or both to a title company, a lender, a home inspector, possibly a moving company, and eventually a string of contractors. Every one of those handoffs is a point where a scammer can insert themselves — and the money involved is large enough that they're actively trying.

This isn't hypothetical risk. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) tracked over 12,000 real estate fraud complaints in 2025 alone, totaling more than $275 million in losses — up from roughly $173 million the year before. That's a steep upward trend, and it's concentrated almost entirely in one specific type of crime: someone pretending to be a party you already trust, at the exact moment you're least likely to double-check.

Here's how to vet the vendors in your transaction properly, and the specific red flags for each type of scam actually hitting Emerald Coast buyers and sellers right now.

---

### The Big One: Wire Fraud at Closing

This is where the FBI's dollar figures above are concentrated, and it's worth understanding the mechanics precisely because the scam is almost always more sophisticated than "an obviously fake email."

**How it actually works:** A scammer gains access to — or spoofs — the email account of your title company, closing attorney, or real estate agent, typically through a prior phishing compromise somewhere in the chain. Days or hours before your closing, you receive an email that looks completely legitimate, on the right letterhead, referencing your actual closing date and property address, informing you of "updated wire instructions" for your closing funds. You wire the money. It goes to the scammer's account, not the title company's, and by the time anyone notices, the funds have usually already moved through several accounts and are effectively unrecoverable.

**The red flags:**

1. **Any change to wire instructions, ever, sent by email.** Legitimate title companies do not change wire instructions last-minute over email. This is the single most reliable signal of fraud in progress.
2. **Urgency language** — "wire today or the closing will be delayed," "final instructions, please confirm receipt immediately." Real closings have buffer time built in; manufactured urgency is a manipulation tactic, not a genuine deadline.
3. **Slightly altered email addresses** — a domain with one letter transposed, or an extra hyphen. These are easy to miss when you're skimming on a phone.

**What to actually do:** Before wiring any closing funds, call your title company using a phone number you already had on file or pulled from their official website — never a number provided in the email containing the wire instructions. Confirm the instructions verbally. This single habit defeats the overwhelming majority of real estate wire fraud attempts, and reputable title companies in Okaloosa County expect and welcome this call — if anyone acts annoyed that you're verifying, that itself is worth paying attention to.

### Vetting Contractors: Especially After Storm Season

Florida's post-hurricane environment creates a predictable pattern: roofing and repair "storm chasers" — often out-of-state crews working door-to-door in affected neighborhoods — show up fast, quote aggressively, and are gone before any warranty issue surfaces.

**How to actually verify a contractor:**

1. **Check their license on the Florida DBPR website** (myfloridalicense.com). Florida requires state certification for most contracting work, and the lookup tool shows license status, any disciplinary history, and whether the license is active or has lapsed.
2. **Ask for proof of general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage directly from the insurer**, not just a certificate the contractor hands you — certificates can be altered or expired without the contractor mentioning it.
3. **Be wary of large upfront deposits.** Florida law (F.S. § 489.126) restricts how much a contractor can require upfront on residential jobs before returning to do the work — legitimate contractors structure payment around project milestones, not a large sum collected before anything starts.
4. **Get multiple bids for anything beyond minor repairs.** A bid dramatically below every other quote is often a sign of corner-cutting materials, unlicensed subcontractors, or a contractor who has no intention of finishing the job.

If a contractor showed up unsolicited after a storm, works out of an out-of-state phone number, and pressures you to sign same-day — that combination alone is reason enough to say no and get a second opinion from a locally established company.

### Vetting Home Inspectors

Florida requires home inspectors to be licensed through the DBPR as well, and the same license lookup tool applies. Beyond confirming licensure, the bigger risk with inspectors is a subtler one: **conflict of interest**.

If a listing agent hands you a single inspector recommendation with no context, ask why. A good listing agent recommends several qualified inspectors, or none at all, and lets you choose independently. An inspector who works almost exclusively through referrals from one particular agent or brokerage has a financial incentive to keep that referral relationship smooth — which occasionally translates into a softer report than an independent inspector might deliver. This isn't universal, and plenty of agent-referred inspectors do excellent, honest work, but it's worth choosing your own inspector from an independent search rather than defaulting to the first name handed to you.

### PCS Movers: The Scam Category Military Families Hit Most

If you're relocating to Eglin AFB or Hurlburt Field, movers are where things go wrong most often — and the scam has a specific, well-documented pattern known as a **"hostage load."**

**How it works:** A moving company gives you a low estimate over the phone without an in-person or video survey of your belongings. On moving day, they load your entire household onto the truck, then present a dramatically higher bill before they'll unload — sometimes at your new home, hundreds of miles from where you started, with your possessions effectively held hostage until you pay.

**How to protect yourself:**

1. **For a government-funded PCS move**, work through your installation's Transportation Office and move.mil rather than sourcing a mover independently — this routes you through vetted carriers with actual accountability to the government contract.
2. **For a self-arranged or partial DITY move**, verify the mover's registration through the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration at protectyourmove.gov, which lists licensed interstate movers and complaint history by USDOT number.
3. **Never accept a binding estimate without an in-person or thorough video walkthrough** of everything being moved — a phone-only estimate has no real basis and is a common precursor to a dramatically inflated final bill.
4. **Get everything in writing**, including the estimated weight, cost per pound over the estimate, and delivery window, before the truck is loaded.

### Rental Scams for Incoming PCS Families

One more pattern specific to military relocation: scammers scrape real listing photos of an actual home — sometimes one that's genuinely for sale, not for rent — and repost them on Facebook Marketplace or Craigslist as a rental, often priced attractively below market to create urgency. They collect a security deposit and first month's rent via wire or gift card from an incoming family who can't tour the property in person before their report date, then disappear.

Before sending any deposit for a property you haven't personally toured: verify actual ownership through the **Okaloosa County Property Appraiser's** public records search. If the "landlord" doesn't match the recorded owner, or refuses a video walkthrough with a live, unedited feed, stop the transaction entirely.

### The Common Thread

Every scam in this article works the same way: someone impersonates a party you already trust, at a moment when speed feels more important than verification. The fix is nearly identical across all of them — independently confirm identity and instructions through a channel you control, not one the other party hands you. It costs you one extra phone call. It's the difference between a normal transaction and a very expensive lesson.
