Most first-time homebuyer guides read like they were written for a generic suburb that could be anywhere in the country. Buying your first home in Okaloosa County has its own specific mechanics — a particular Florida contract form, an insurance market that behaves nothing like most of the country, and a few procedural changes from the last couple of years that a lot of first-timers still don't know about. Here's the version written for what you'll actually run into.

---

### Get Pre-Approved, Not Pre-Qualified — and Know Your Real Number

These two terms get used interchangeably and they shouldn't be. A pre-qualification is a rough estimate based on numbers you self-reported. A **pre-approval** means a lender has actually pulled your credit and reviewed real documentation — pay stubs, bank statements, tax returns — and is telling you a number they've actually underwritten, not guessed at. In a market where competing offers are common, a seller's agent can usually tell the difference, and a self-reported pre-qualification carries far less weight.

If this is genuinely your first home, look into Florida-specific down payment assistance before assuming you need 20% down. **Florida Housing's homebuyer programs** — including **Florida Hometown Heroes**, which offers additional assistance for eligible community workforce buyers (teachers, healthcare workers, law enforcement, and military, among others) — can meaningfully change what's realistic. Program terms and loan caps shift over time, so get current numbers from a participating lender rather than relying on anything you read online, including this.

### You'll Need to Sign a Buyer Agreement Before You Tour Homes

This is the part that trips up more first-time buyers than almost anything else, simply because it's a relatively recent change: since **August 2024**, real estate agents nationwide are required to have a **signed written buyer representation agreement** in place before showing you homes, following the industry-wide settlement that restructured how agent compensation works. If an agent asks you to sign something before your first showing, that's not a sales tactic — it's now how the process legally works everywhere, including here.

Read what you're signing. These agreements specify the agent's compensation and the length of the representation term — both are negotiable, and a first-time buyer shouldn't sign the first version handed to them without understanding what it commits you to.

### The FAR/BAR "AS-IS" Contract and Your Inspection Period

Florida real estate contracts almost universally use the **FAR/BAR "AS-IS" Residential Contract for Sale and Purchase** — a standardized form jointly developed by the Florida Realtors and The Florida Bar. "AS-IS" doesn't mean you can't inspect the home or negotiate; it means the seller isn't obligated to make any repairs, and your leverage comes entirely from your **inspection period** — typically negotiated around 10 to 15 days from contract execution.

During that window, you have the right to cancel the contract for any reason (or no stated reason at all) and get your earnest money deposit back in full. This makes the inspection period the single most important stretch of your entire purchase, and it's where a lot of first-time buyers under-invest — treating a home inspection as a formality rather than the actual due-diligence window it is.

**Earnest money deposits** in this market commonly run 1–3% of the purchase price, sometimes higher in a competitive multiple-offer situation. It's held in escrow, typically by the title company or a brokerage's escrow account, and gets credited toward your down payment and closing costs at closing.

### Get Real Insurance Quotes During the Inspection Period — Not After

This is the single biggest first-timer mistake specific to this coast, and it's avoidable. Homeowners insurance here isn't a rounding error the way it might be in much of the country — coastal windstorm exposure means premiums can vary dramatically based on a home's construction, roof age, and wind mitigation features. Buyers who wait until after their inspection period closes to get a real insurance quote sometimes discover the actual annual premium is thousands more than they budgeted, with no contractual way left to back out cleanly.

Request actual insurance quotes — not ballpark estimates — during your inspection period, using the property's actual wind mitigation inspection report if the seller has a current one (or getting one done yourself; it's a modest cost that can unlock four- and five-figure annual savings depending on the home's roof-to-wall connections and roof age). Also confirm the property's flood zone designation before your financing contingency runs out — a federally backed loan on a high-risk flood zone property requires flood insurance, and that's a cost that needs to be in your numbers from day one, not discovered at the closing table.

### Closing Costs Nobody Warns First-Timers About

Budget for roughly **2–5% of the purchase price** in closing costs beyond your down payment, and know specifically what's in that number:

1. **Documentary stamp tax on the deed** — a Florida transfer tax, customarily paid by the seller, calculated on the sale price.
2. **Title insurance** — in much of the Florida Panhandle, including Okaloosa County, it's customary (though negotiable, not statutory) for the **buyer** to pay for the owner's title insurance policy, which is the opposite convention from South Florida. Don't assume the norm you read about online applies here — ask your title company directly what's customary in this specific market.
3. **Prorated property taxes** — you'll reimburse the seller for taxes they've already paid covering your portion of the year, calculated off the **seller's current assessed value**, which may be significantly lower than what your own tax bill becomes once the property reassesses to market value the following year and your own homestead exemption timeline starts fresh. Don't budget your ongoing housing cost off the seller's old tax bill.
4. **Lender fees, appraisal, and recording fees** — standard across any financed purchase, itemized on your Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing under federal TRID rules.

### What Actually Happens at Closing

By the time you get to the closing table, the heavy lifting should already be done: financing finalized, inspection contingencies resolved, insurance bound and paid for the first year (most lenders require proof of paid insurance before funding). What's left is signing a stack of documents, wiring your remaining funds — **always verified by phone through a number you already had, never through instructions emailed to you**, since real estate wire fraud specifically targets this exact moment — and getting your keys.

---

### A Practical First-Timer's Checklist

1. **Get a real, underwritten pre-approval**, not a rough estimate, before you start touring homes seriously.
2. **Understand the buyer agreement** before you sign one — compensation terms and length are negotiable.
3. **Use your full inspection period** — it's your only real leverage in an AS-IS contract, and it exists for a reason.
4. **Get actual insurance quotes during that window**, not after, using the home's real wind mitigation status.
5. **Confirm the flood zone** and what insurance it requires before your financing contingency expires.
6. **Ask specifically what's customary here** on closing cost splits — Panhandle norms differ from other parts of Florida.
7. **Verify wire instructions by phone**, every time, no exceptions.

None of this is complicated once you know it's coming. The mistake first-time buyers make isn't lack of intelligence — it's assuming the process works the same way everywhere, when a handful of specific, local details are exactly where the real money sits.
